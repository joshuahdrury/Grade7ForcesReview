Part A: External Forces & How We Describe Them

An external force acts on a structure from the outside. Engineers classify external forces by what causes them and how they hit the structure.

1. Gravity (Non-Contact Force) ⬇️

The downward pull of Earth on all mass. It acts constantly on every part of a structure, pulling it toward Earth's center.

2. Wind & Seismic (Earthquake) Forces 💨

Moving air pushes horizontally against walls and roofs, while earthquakes shake the ground beneath foundations back and forth.

3. Magnitude & Direction 📏

Magnitude is the size/strength of a force (measured in Newtons, N). Direction is which way it pushes or pulls (shown by arrow orientation).

4. Point & Plane of Application 🎯

Point of application is the exact spot where a force hits. Plane of application is the flat surface/angle (e.g., 90° side vs. top) receiving the force.

Gravity: Always pulls straight downward toward Earth's center of mass.

Part B: Types of Loads on a Structure

A load is any external force acting on a structure. Engineers divide loads into three categories:

1. Dead Load (Static Load) 🧱

The permanent weight of the structure itself—beams, concrete, roof shingles, bricks, and permanently attached pipes. It never moves.

2. Live Load (Dynamic Load) 🚛

Temporary or moving weight that a structure supports—people, furniture, parked or moving cars, books, and cargo.

3. Environmental Load ❄️

Forces caused by weather and nature—heavy snow accumulation on a roof, strong wind gusts, freezing rain/ice, or seismic tremors.

Dead Load: The steel girders, concrete deck, and cables of the bridge itself.

Part C: The Four Internal Forces

When external loads push or pull on a structure, they create internal forces inside the building materials themselves.

1. Tension (Pulling / Stretching) ↔️

An internal force that pulls particles of a material apart, stretching it. Example: Suspension bridge cables or a guitar string.

2. Compression (Pushing / Squeezing) ➡️⬅️

An internal force that presses or squeezes particles together. Example: Stone pillars supporting a temple or chair legs under you.

3. Shear (Sliding / Tearing) ✂️

Forces acting in opposite parallel directions, causing layers of material to slide past each other or snap. Example: Scissors cutting paper or bolts shearing.

4. Torsion (Twisting) 🔄

An internal twisting force created when opposite ends of a structure rotate in different directions. Example: Wringing a wet cloth or wind twisting a bridge.

Tension: Particles are pulled apart along the length of the cable.
